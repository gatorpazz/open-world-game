## Function

The sky-priced zone: the old surface world of terraces, granaries, and
workshops abandoned at the Coming, now the salvage economy's field and
the exploration pillar's sharpest edge — fast, silent, unbearably
valuable, and roofless.

## Canon summary

The Open Fields are the island's former face: stepped grain terraces,
courtyard farmsteads, cistern towers, and lanes still legible under
scrub. The Coming emptied them in a season. What could be carried went
under; what could not — mills, glazed windows, seed stores, whole
libraries of daylight life — waits where it was left, weathering
slowly. Salvage crews from [[lastdoor]] work them in timed sprints
between covers, by rules written in exposure tallies and paid for in
names.

## Visible traits

Hard blue daylight. Stillness with wind in it. Terrace walls gone
gold-grey, doorways without doors, a plow standing in a furrow it will
never finish. From any rise: the shuttered face of [[highroof]] and,
above the cloud, sometimes, a shape.

## Systems impact

The salvage economy's source: surface goods carry scarcity prices
(economy pass). Traversal is the roofed/open loop at maximum stakes —
cover-to-cover movement, exposure budgets, no flight, no shouting.
Wardens license entry through [[lastdoor]] and log every minute of sky.

## Relationships

Entered by [[lastdoor]]'s Last Gate; watched from the [[bait_square]];
holds the ruins the timeline pass will hang its oldest surface events
on. Under [[the_taking]] absolutely.

## Quest hooks

- A crew's exposure budget ran out one roof short of home; they are
  alive, under cover, and the rescue question is now a town argument.
- A granary's seal is intact but its seed is gone — removed carefully,
  recently, from the inside.
- A courtyard farmstead's grief-tablets are still mounted — and one
  family in Lastdoor pays quietly to keep anyone from salvaging that
  particular house.

## Environmental storytelling

Doorways facing the sky bricked half-up in the last panicked season.
Chalk cover-marks — salvager wayfinding — layered over older, finer
housepaint. A child's whirligig on a sill, motionless until the wind.
The silence that is not empty.
